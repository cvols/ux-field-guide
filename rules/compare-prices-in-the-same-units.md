---
title: Compare prices in the same units
summary: Show an add-on's price beside what it relates to, in comparable terms, and never use a comparison that makes it look smaller than it is.
category: choice
principle: Anchoring
confidence: disputed
sources: [yt-2TlIg3VokY8]
---

**Do:** Show an add-on next to the item it covers, with both prices in the
same terms, such as per year or total cost. Leave the add-on unselected,
with a plainly labeled way to decline.

**Don't:** Compare a monthly fee with a one-off price, add decoy tiers that
nobody should buy, invent reference prices, or put add-ons in the basket
before people choose them.

**Why:** People judge a price against whatever they saw just before it.
That makes the comparison powerful, and easy to misuse.

**Notes:** Anchoring held up in the Many Labs 1 replication (Klein et al.,
2014), but decoy effects are fragile with realistic displays (Frederick, Lee,
and Baskin, 2014)
(https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/The-Limits-of-Attraction.pdf).
The EU bans pre-ticked boxes for extra payments. yt-2TlIg3VokY8 (9:36) shows
a $49 monthly protection plan as "just 2.6%" of a $1,899 laptop. A year of
the plan costs $588, about 31% of the laptop, so the example breaks this
rule. Disputed because the evidence on how much price context changes
choices is mixed. The rule's ethics hold either way.
